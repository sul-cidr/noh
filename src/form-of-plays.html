Form of the Plays

The Zeami/Yokomichi model is a reference rather than an absolute, since few plays follow it exactly. Hashitomi and Kokaji’s form underlines this point, since both deviate from it somewhat.

In the following two figures, the section's name is linked to the beginning of that section within the play, while the shōdan are linked to their respective intermedia analysis. Not shown in the model but present in both plays is the kyōgen interlude. This is often performed between the two acts but there are no shōdan associated with it.

Takasago

This figure summarizes the formal structure of Takasago. It follows the conventional two-part structure of Noh, associated with Zeami and later formalized analytically by Yokomichi. Yet it transforms the usual mugen Noh pattern of concealed identity and revelation into a waki Noh celebration, with the dialogue omitted from the second act. Its unity is symbolic and ritual rather than suspenseful: the play leads not to the discovery of a tragic past but to the public manifestation of harmony and divine favor.

Okina

This figure summarizes the formal structure of Okina. Because it has no plot, no conventional waki–shite dramatic encounter, and no concealed identity revealed in a second act, its structure does not fit neatly within Yokomichi’s model.

Although Okina predates Zeami, Zeami’s principle of jo–ha–kyū can be discerned in its structure, albeit only in broad terms. Okina begins in a slow, solemn, and ceremonial mode (jo); acquires movement and energy through the dances (ha); before reaching its most vigorous and accelerated conclusion in Sanbasō (kyū). For this reason, we have chosen to consider it in relation to the framework of the two-act Noh play: a first “act” centered on Okina and a second centered on Sanbasō, separated by Furyū, a kyōgen interlude.
