--- title: OKINA ---

Synopsis

Although Okina is often referred to as a Noh play, strictly speaking it should not be classified as a play. Its origins actually precede the era of Zeami, the playwright who elevated Noh to the esteemed art form recognized today.Okina functions as a kind of divine service, intended to pray for peace in the world and the nation. Accordingly, the play does not have a conventional plot. It is often performed prior to another play and is traditionally used to celebrate auspicious occasions, such as New Year’s Day, the opening of new theatres, or the relocation of a university, as is the case with the present recording.

Before the performance, each actor must undergo a purification ritual. Today, only the principal actor observes the seven-day process; for supporting actors and musicians, purification generally occurs on the day of the performance. Typically, an altar is set up in the mirror room for this purpose. The boxes containing the okina and kokushiki-jō masks, along with stage properties, are placed there together with various offerings. It is customary for the performers to gather and partake in a ritual libation of sake for purification, followed by the ceremonial striking of flint to produce purifying sparks.

Intermedia Form

The play’s introduction is followed by three sections that we labeled: Okina, Furyū, and Sanbasō. The Furyū refers to a special, more celebratory version of the Okina ritual. It adds a grander and more festive dimension, featuring an additional deity or spirit who appears between the Okina’s dance and Sanbasō’s dance.

The Section Map under the video represents the play’s intermedia form. It also shows that the play is composed of three acts, and that the first one contains fifteen sections, the second one has seven, while the third one has five sections.

The background color groups the three sections’ subsections. The first section has seven sub-sections, the second one has three, while the third section has four. Bringing the cursor over a sub-section identifies the section to which it belongs.

A comparison between the duration of the three sections shows that the second section is the shortest, and that the first is slightly longer than the third section. It is noteworthy that the second section has been added for this special performance.

The height of the blocks shows the development of the play’s Index of Intermedia (IoI). The different heights demonstrate that the development is not linear but comes in waves. We have identified three kinds of waves.

First: the rising wave within a subsection. In the first and second sections, each subsection’s IoI forms a wave leading to a peak closer to its end, suggesting that the relationship between the media complexifies as the subsection is unfolding.

Second: the rising wave across a section. The way the IoI transforms over each section differs substantially. The first and third ones count two major waves, whereas there is only one in the second section, as the IoI of subsequent dan increases until the noriji-2.

Third: the rising wave of the play as a whole. The IoI is at its lowest at the beginning of the first section, reaching its highest IoI in its sixth subsection, with Okina’s instrumental dance. The lower IoI, at the beginning of the second section, increases but without reaching the first section’s highest level. In fact, the highest IoI is found in the third and last section with Sanbasō two instrumental dances
