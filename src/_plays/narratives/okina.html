--- title: OKINA ---

Synopsis

Although Okina is classified as a Noh play, it is not considered a genuine Noh play. Its origins actually precede the era of Zeami, the playwright who elevated Noh to the esteemed art form recognized today.

Okina functions as a kind of divine service, intended to pray for peace in the world and the nation. Accordingly, the play does not have a conventional plot. It is often performed prior to another play and is traditionally used to celebrate auspicious occasions, such as New Year’s Day, the opening of new theatres, or the relocation of a university, as is the case with the present recording.

Before the performance, each actor must undergo a purification ritual. Today, only the principal actor observes the seven-day process; for supporting actors and musicians, purification generally occurs on the day of the performance. Typically, an altar is set up in the mirror room for this purpose. The boxes containing the okina and kokushiki-jō masks, along with stage properties, are placed there together with various offerings. It is customary for the performers to gather and partake in a ritual libation of sake for purification, followed by the ceremonial striking of flint to produce purifying sparks.

Intermedia Form

The play’s introduction is followed by three sections that we labeled: Okina, Furyū, and Sanbasō. The Furyū refers to a special, more celebratory version of the Okina ritual.It adds a grander and more festive dimension, featuring an additional deity or spirit who appears after the Okina’s dance but before and Sanbasō’s dance.

The Shōdan Map under the video represents the play’s intermedia form. It also shows that the play is composed of three acts, and that the first one contains fifteen sections, the second one has seven, while the third one has five sections.

The background color groups the three sections’ subsections. The first section has seven sub-sections, the second one has three, while the third section has four. Bringing the cursor over a sub-section identifies the section to which it belongs.

A comparison between the duration of the three sections shows that the second section is the shortest, and that the first is slightly longer than the third section. It is noteworthy that the second section has been added for this special performance.

The height of the blocks shows the development of the play’s Index of Intermedia (IoI). The different heights demonstrate that the development is not linear but comes in waves. We have identified three kinds of waves.

First: the rising wave within a subsection. In the first and second sections, each subsection’s IoI forms a wave leading to a peak closer to its end, suggesting that the relationship between the media complexifies as the subsection is unfolding.

Second: the rising wave across a section. The way the IoI transforms over each section differs substantially. The first and third ones count two major waves, whereas there is only one in the second section, as the IoI of subsequent dan increases until the noriji-2.

Third: the rising wave of the play as a whole. The IoI is at its lowest at the beginning of the first section, reaching its highest IoI in its sixth subsection, with Okina’s instrumental dance. The lower IoI, at the beginning of the second section, increases but without reaching the first section’s highest level. In fact, the highest IoI is found in the third and last section with Sanbasō two instrumental dances

Okina

Procession

Composed of two sub-sections, this section aims at setting the characters on stage.

The procession opens with Watari, a ritualistic procession that brings on stage two of the play’s three main characters: Senzai and Okina-to-be. Sanbasō, the third main character, and the musicians wait on stage until the section’s conclusion to proceed to their respective stage position.

The next section, the Zatsuki-bue is a non-congruent nohkan solo performed as everybody is settling on stage. It sets a solemn and mystical atmosphere, heightening the ceremonial nature of the performance.

The procession essentially acts as a preparation to the play’s first songs and as such its Index of Intermedia is low.

Introductory songs

This section introduces the first two songs of the play. Evoking a mantra, the texts of Song-2, as well as the initial lines of Song-1, are rendered in an archaic language, imbuing the section with a spiritual quality. This effect is further heightened by the fact that the Okina-to-be remains seated at the incidental Square 7 while reciting his lines, conveying a sense of meditative contemplation.

Supported by the three kotsuzumi in strict rhythmic setting, and a non-congruent nohkan part, the Index of Intermedia of these two chants for two voices registers a modest increase, thereby initiating the play’s dramatic progression while inviting further developments.

Senzai performs

This section is comprised of four sub-sections, including two songs, each followed by a corresponding dance. The focus shifts from Okina-to-be to Senzai, Okina’s young attendant. The instrumental accompaniment remains consistent across the two songs and dances, featuring the three kotsuzumi in a strict rhythmic setting juxtaposed with a non-congruent nohkan part.

Both songs are examples of dance-to-text performance. In Song 1, Senzai and the jiutai deliver a warning regarding the unpredictable power of nature and the potential presence of malevolent spirits. This theme is revisited in Song 2, which follows with a plea for a millennium of blessings.

During Dance 1, Senzai's kata are primarily performed center stage, specifically in Squares 4 and 9. The choreography centers on swirling sleeves and stamping footwork. Dance 2 is largely a variation of Dance 1; however, a key difference lies in the fact that Okina-to-be discreetly dons his white mask during this dance, thereby transforming into Okina.

The section presents two paired dance-to-text performances and instrumental dances, resulting in two short, peaking waves in the Index to Intermedia.

Encounter

The Encounter section is not subdivided. Despite its brevity, this section holds significance as Okina establishes contact with Sanbasō-to-be. For the first time, the two figures rise and advance toward one another.

This section also marks the initial entrance of the ōtsuzumi, who produces off-beat strokes, seemingly in an attempt to counteract the pattern of the kotsuzumi. The performer executes his part while remaining seated on the floor, appearing to suggest that his role has not yet formally commenced.

Okina Encounter

Okina prays

Okina's prayer, interrupted by a response, delineates this section into three sub-sections.

Prayer 1, delivered unaccompanied from Square 8 upstage, constitutes a brief celebration of the deities.

This prayer is interrupted by a succinct Response from the jiutai, supported by the lead kotsuzumi player, with a part in flexible rhythmic setting and a non-congruent nohkan part, acknowledging Okina’s pronouncement.

Prayer 2 ensues, returning to an unaccompanied chant as Okina resumes his invocations to the deities.

The aforementioned sequence of sub-sections momentarily reduces the intensity of the Index of Intermedia, providing a respite before the resumption of further dances.

Okina performs

This section is structured into three sub-sections, with the first and third categorized as dance-to-text sub-sections, flanking an instrumental dance. The three dances are supported by the three kotsuzumi, joined by the nohkan for the initial two.

Dance 1 marks Okina's inaugural dance, during which he expands the stage space, as he and the jiutai contemplate, "Who are these old men?" referencing the Encounter section.

Dance 2 appears to unfold on a distinct temporal plane, wherein the deity Okina seems to move in decelerated motion, synchronized with the kotsuzumi’s strokes.

In Dance 3, Okina seemingly confronts the prospect of reverting to his former persona as an old man. Consequently, this dance features restricted movement, with Okina executing all of his kata while positioned upstage on Square 8, invoking, alongside the jiutai, a ten-thousand-year blessing. The cadential character of this dance-to-text is further accentuated by the reduced instrumentation, with the nohkan designated tacet.

Exit

The Exit section is not subdivided. After removing the mask, Okina returns to be an old man. He bows to the audience and slowly departs the stage guided by the accelerating rhythm of the three kotsuzumi.

While the absence of dance results in a lower Index of Intermedia, the choreographed exit is still assessed favorably.

Furyū

Senzai Furyū enters

Composed of three sub-sections, this section sets the stage for the arrival of a new character specific to this performance of Okina, Senzai Furyū. The first two sub-sections contribute to a brief rise in the Index of Intermedia; this increase is then reversed, with the much-lowered Index in the third sub-section.

The entrance music that welcomes him to the stage, the watari byōshi, features the first congruent nohkan part, accompanied by the ōtsuzumi, lead kotsuzumi, and the taiko, which joins the hayashi.

The nohkan is silent during the noriji-1 that follows, which introduces an einori chant celebrating a peaceful reign.

During the nanori that concludes the section, Senzai Furyū reminds the audience that this performance of Okina is specifically presented to celebrate the relocation of the Kyoto City University of Arts.

Furyū enters

This section is composed of two sub-sections. The first one completes the decreasing motion of the Index of Intermedia before initiating its ascent again.

The first sub-section is a brief eentrance music performed exclusively by the ōtsuzumi and lead kotsuzumi players. It introduces the characters Pine and Bamboo, who deliver the issei chant that follows.

Furyū performs

This section is composed of two sub-sections. The first decreases the Index of Intermedia before initiating its ascent again.

The first sub-section is a mondō. During this unaccompanied dialogue, Bamboo and Pine remain on the Bridge, informing Furyū Senzai of their arrival to celebrate, through music and dance, the relocation of the Kyoto City University of Arts.

The noriji-2 that follows introduces a dance-to text sub-section. Supported solely by the ōtsuzzumi and lead kotsuzumi players, the two actors performs a series synchronized kata that either mimic the text or reinforce it with symbolism.

Sanbasō

Kyōgen performs

Comprising three sub-sections, this section prepares the stage for the entrance of Sanbasō-to-be.

The Momidashi dance-to-text extends the rising motion of the Index of Intermedia initiated in the preceding section. The spoken text, supported by the three kotsuzumi and ōtsuzumi in strict rhythmic setting, and a non-congruent nohkan part, expresses wishes for universal happiness.

This is followed by the instrumental dance, Mominodan, which culminates in the peak of the Index of Intermedia. This dance, divided into seven parts, is characterized by stamping footwork, swirling sleeves, and stage motion between squares, 3, 5, and 8

The segment concludes with the character of Sanbasō-to-be inconspicuously donning the Kokushiki-jō mask, thereby transforming into Sanbasō, a deity of festive dance and purification.

Sanbasō performs

This section is divided into three sub-sections. While the Index of Intermedia remains relatively low in the first and third sub-sections, the second features an instrumental dance that elevates it to its highest level for the second time.

The initial sub-section presents a mondō, a dialogue between Sanbasō and Senzai. In this exchange, Senzai encourages Sanbasō to perform a dance as a prayer for a ten-thousand-year blessing.

After receiving a suzu bell from Senzai, Sanbasō performs a dance accompanied by the ōtsuzumi and three kotsuzumi, in strict rhythmic setting and a congruent nohan part. This five-section dance is choreographed around the ringing of the suzu bell on beats 1, 3, 5, and 7.

In the final sub-section, Sanbasō removes his mask, reverting to the character of an old man.
