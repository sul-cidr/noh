--- title: OKINA ---

Synopsis

Although Okina is often referred to as a Noh play, strictly speaking it should not be classified as a play. Its origins actually precede the era of Zeami, the playwright who elevated Noh to the esteemed art form recognized today. Okina functions as a kind of divine service, intended to pray for peace in the world and the nation. Accordingly, the play does not have a conventional plot. It is often performed prior to another play and is traditionally used to celebrate auspicious occasions, such as New Year’s Day, the opening of new theatres or – as is the case with the present recording – the relocation of a university, .

Before the performance, each actor must undergo a purification ritual. Today, only the principal actor observes the seven-day process; for supporting actors and musicians, purification generally occurs on the day of the performance. Typically, an altar is set up in the mirror room for this purpose. The boxes containing the okina and kokushiki-jō masks, along with stage properties, are placed there together with various offerings. It is customary for the performers to gather and partake in a ritual drinking of sake for purification, followed by the ceremonial striking of flint to produce purifying sparks.

Intermedia Form

The play’s introduction is followed by two acts labeled: Okina and Sanbasō, separated by the interlude called Furyū. Its presence marks a special, more celebratory version of the Okina ritual. Furyū adds a grander and more festive dimension, featuring an additional deity or spirit.

The Shōdan Map under the video represents the play’s intermedia form. It shows the three part structure and that the first act contains fifteen shōdan, the interlude has seven, and the second act has six shōdan.

The background color groups the shōdan into dan. The first act has seven dan, the interlude has three, while the second act has four. Bringing the cursor over a shōdan identifies its name and the dan to which it belongs.

A comparison between the duration of the three large sections shows that the interlude is the shortest, and that the first act is slightly longer than the second act.

The height of the blocks shows the development of the play’s Index of Intermedia (IoI). The different heights demonstrate that the development is not linear but comes in waves. We have identified three kinds of waves.

First: the rising wave within a dan. In the first act and the interlude, each dan IoI forms a wave leading to a peak closer to its end, suggesting that the relationship between the media complexifies as the dan is unfolding.

Second: the rising wave across an act. The way the IoI transforms over each act differs substantially. The first and second acts count two major waves, whereas there is only one in the interlude, as the IoI of subsequent dan increases until the noriji-2.

Third: the rising wave of the play as a whole. The IoI is at its lowest at the beginning of the first act, reaching its highest IoI in its sixth dan, with Okina’s instrumental dance. The lower IoI, at the beginning of the interlude, increases but without reaching the first act's highest level. In fact, the highest IoI is found in the second act with Sanbasō's two instrumental dances

Okina

Procession

Composed of two shōdan, this dan aims at setting the characters on stage.

The procession opens with Watari, a ritualistic procession that brings on stage two of the play’s three main characters: Senzai and Okina-to-be. Sanbasō, the third main character, and the musicians wait on stage until the shōdan's conclusion to proceed to their respective stage position.

The next shōdan, the Zatsuki-bue is a non-congruent nohkan solo performed as everybody is settling on stage. It sets a solemn and mystical atmosphere, heightening the ceremonial nature of the performance.

The procession only acts as a preparation to the play’s first songs and as such its Index of Intermedia is low.

Introductory Song

This dan introduces the first song of the play. Evoking a mantra, the texts is rendered in an archaic language, imbuing the section with a spiritual quality. This effect is further heightened by the fact that the Okina-to-be remains seated at the incidental Square 7 while reciting his lines, conveying a contemplative spirit.

Supported by the three kotsuzumi in strict rhythmic setting, and a non-congruent nohkan part, the Index of Intermedia of the chant for two voices shows a modest increase, thereby starting the play’s dramatic progression towards further developments.

Okina Song

Senzai performs

This dan focuses on Senzai, Okina’s young attendant. Typically portrayed as a younger figure, Senzai brings youthful energy and exuberance to the performance. His part is comprised of four shōdan, organized as two pairs: a song followed by its corresponding dance.

The songs celebrate the power of water, describing how its force persists even under the weakening sun. The dances then physically illustrate this theme. In Song 1, Senzai and the jiutai sing of this enduring power; in Song 2, this evolves into a plea for a millennium of blessings. While Senzai performs Dance 2, the performer who will be Okina discreetly puts on his white mask, transforming into the deity.

The instrumentation distinguishes the songs from the dances. The songs are sung by Senzai and the jiutai, accompanied only by the three kotsuzumi. In the dances, the singing is replaced by the nohkan flute, which raises the Index of Intermedia from medium (song) to high (dance). There is also a noticeable acceleration throughout the dan. While the dances are about two-fifths longer than the songs, and the first pair of shōdan is considerably longer than the second, the second pair is performed at a much faster tempo. This culminates in Dance 2, the expressive climax of the dan. It is louder, featuring multiple foot stamps, and more energetic, with the speed of the dance increasing and the musical tempo doubling.

Encounter

Though brief and not subdivided, the ‘Encounter’ dan is of great significance. It presents the pivotal first meeting of the play's two contrasting central deities: the dignified Okina and the robust, earthbound Sanbasō. This charged meeting maintains a high Index of Intermedia, brought to life through a rich interplay of chanting, stage movement, and the full hayashi ensemble, which features the first entrance of the ōtsuzumi drum.

Intermedia analysis:
Okina Encounter

Okina prays

This dan forms the central prayer of the Okina act, structured as a three-part dialogue between Okina and the jiutai. It begins with Okina’s first prayer, delivered as a slow, unaccompanied incantation. The jiutai, accompanied by instruments, then offers a choral response, raising temporarily the Index of Intermedia while maintaining the solemn, prayer-like atmosphere. The dan culminates with Okina’s second prayer, which begins introspectively but builds to a powerful proclamation for peace and security. Visually, the focus remains on Okina, whose contained and deliberate gestures—such as slowly opening his arms or covering his face with a fan—heighten the sense of sacred ritual.

The dan temporarily reduces the Index of Intermedia, providing a respite before the resumption of further dances. More importantly, it functions as a thematic prelude, establishing the symbolism of the three poles—Heaven, Earth, and Man—which, along longevity, peace, and celebration, will now be explored in the dance dan that follows

Okina performs

This dan is structured into three shōdan, with the first and third categorized as dance-to-text shōdan, flanking an instrumental dance. The three dances are supported by the three kotsuzumi, joined by the nohkan for the initial two.

The structure of Okina's instrumental dance is based on three sequences of stamps named Heaven, Earth, and Man, respectively.

Dance 1 marks Okina's inaugural dance, during which he expands the stage space, as he and the jiutai contemplate, Who are these old men? referencing the Encounter dan.

Dance 2 appears to unfold on a distinct temporal plane, wherein the deity Okina seems to move in decelerated motion, synchronized with the kotsuzumi’s strokes.

In Dance 3, This dance features restricted movement, with Okina executing all of his kata while positioned upstage on Square 8, invoking, alongside the jiutai, a ten-thousand-year blessing. The cadential character of this dance-to-text is further accentuated by the reduced instrumentation, with the nohkan absent.

Exit

The Exit dan is not subdivided. After removing the mask, Okina returns to be an old man. He bows to the audience and slowly departs the stage guided by the accelerating rhythm of the three kotsuzumi.

While the absence of dance results in a lower Index of Intermedia, the choreographed exit is deliberately vivid.

Furyū

Furyūsenzai Enters

Composed of three shōdan, this dan sets the stage for the arrival of a new character specific to this performance of Okina, Furyūsenzai. The first two shōdan contribute to a brief rise in the Index of Intermedia; this increase is then reversed, with the much-lowered Index in the third shōdan.

The entrance music that welcomes Furyūsenzai to the stage, the watari byōshi, features the first congruent nohkan part as well as the first use of taiko in the play. They join the ōtsuzumi and the lead kotsuzumi.

The nohkan is silent during the noriji-1 that follows, which introduces an einori chant celebrating a peaceful reign.

During the nanori that concludes the dan, Furyūsenzai reminds the audience that this performance of Okina is specifically presented to celebrate the relocation of the Kyoto City University of Arts.

Furyū Enters

This dan is composed of two shōdan. The first one brings a decrease, while the second, an increase of the Index of Intermedia.

The first shōdan is a brief entrance music performed exclusively by the ōtsuzumi and lead kotsuzumi players. It introduces the characters; Pine and Bamboo, who will thereafter deliver the issei chant.

Furyū performs

This dan is composed of two shōdan. The first one brings a decrease, while the second, an increase of the Index of Intermedia.

The first shōdan is a mondō. During this unaccompanied dialogue, Bamboo and Pine remain on the Bridge, informing Furyū Senzai of their arrival to celebrate through music and dance the relocation of the Kyoto City University of Arts.

The noriji-2 that follows introduces a dance-to text shōdan. Supported solely by the ōtsuzumi and the lead kotsuzumi player, the two actors perform a series of synchronized kata that either mimic the text or reinforce its symbolism.

Sanbasō

Kyōgen performs

Comprising three shōdan, this dan prepares the stage for the entrance of Sanbasō-to-be.

The Momidashi dance-to-text extends the rising motion of the Index of Intermedia initiated in the preceding dan. The spoken text, supported by the three kotsuzumi and ōtsuzumi in strict rhythmic setting, and a non-congruent nohkan part, expresses wishes for universal happiness.

This is followed by the instrumental dance, Mominodan, which culminates in the peak of the Index of Intermedia. This dance, divided into seven parts, is characterized by stamping footwork, swirling sleeves, and stage motion between squares 3, 5, and 8.

The segment concludes with the character of Sanbasō-to-be inconspicuously putting on the Kokushiki-jō mask, thereby transforming into Sanbasō, a deity of festive dance and purification.

Sanbasō Encounter

This one-shōdan dan is a mondō, a dialogue between Sanbasō and Senzai representing a low Index of Intermedia. In their exchange, Senzai encourages Sanbasō to perform a dance as a prayer for a ten-thousand-year blessing.

Intermedia analysis:
Mondō 2

Sanbasō performs

This dan is divided into two shōdan. The first one features an instrumental dance that elevates the Index of Intermedia to its highest level for the second time during the play. After receiving a suzu bell from Senzai, Sanbasō performs a dance accompanied by the ōtsuzumi and three kotsuzumi, in strict rhythmic setting and a congruent nohan part. This five-section dance is choreographed around the ringing of the suzu bell on beats 1, 3, 5, and 7.

In the final shōdan, Sanbasō removes his mask, symbolically shedding his divine identity.
