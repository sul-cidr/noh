Form
Sanbasō-Kyōgen Performs:
Momidashi → Mominodan → Put the mask on
Synopsis
Momidashi, the opening shōdan of the second act, brings Sanbasō onto the stage in three parts: an instrumental entrance, an extended seated interlude, and a final dance-to-text. His words are an unguarded exclamation of joy and good fortune.
Text
Prose.
Music
The ōtsuzumi and three kotsuzumi parts are in strict rhythmic setting. The nohkan, present only the first part, is non-congruent.
Visuals
As the shōdan begins, the chorus of kyōgen actors who accompanied the Furyū leaves the stage by the bridge. Sanbasō-to-be, who kneels at Square 1 for the first two-thirds of the shōdan, stands up for the last third and dances almost entirely on the left side of the stage on Squares 1, 2, and 3.
Index of Intermedia

The overall Index of Intermedia (IoI) of the first dan of the second act consists of three shōdan, maintaining the IoI at its highest level for most of the section.

The first shōdan features both a spoken part and a dance performed by Sanbasō, accompanied by the ōtsuzumi, and three kotsuzumi in a strict rhythmic setting and a non-congruent nohkan part. Sanbasō, who remains seated at Square 1 for the first two-thirds of the shōdan, finally stands up and performs a series of kata on the stage’s left side. Although this combination slightly lowers the IoI, it keeps it within the higher range.


Momidashi is the opening shōdan of the second act, Sanbasō - Black Mask, and a very powerful one. As a dance-to-text accompanied by the full hayashi, it's Index of Intermedia is among the highest in the play. The intermedia expression is further defined by a highly energetic expression balanced, at a few key moments, by dramatic contrasts of stillness and silence — a push and pull that gives the shōdan its unique quality.

It opens on a transitory feeling: the chorus of kyōgen actors who accompanied the Furyū leaves the bridge. Sanbasō, who has sat with his back to the audience, an almost absent presence upstage since the Encounter in the first act, and throughout the Furyū, is now re-entering the play. He turns around and, as if summoned by the nohkan's an accelerating percussion that gradually establishes a powerful driving flow – while the three kotsuzumi drums keep a steady four-beat pattern, the ōtsuzumi adds strokes between the beats, creating the most dynamic rhythmic layering in the play so far. The nohkan's hishigi pattern, helps assert his presence as he walks briskly forward to Square 3 to perform a Forward point. Then, he takes nine steps back to Square 1, where he remains seated until the final section of the shōdan.

The Forward point and vigorous backward steps are supported by the music: the nohkan’s momidashi pattern concludes with rapid ‘uta’ sounds as the ōtsuzumi drum adds a series of chon strokes. This combination brings the opening of the section’s seven honji to an exhilarating close.

For nearly a minute, the music then runs on its own — entirely instrumental, strictly pulsatile, and among the fastest and rhythmically densest passages to be found anywhere in Noh. Sanbasō, however, does not move. Seated motionless at Square 1 facing the audience, he offers a contrast: not an absence, but a motionless and powerful presence, as if storing the energy the music builds.

That gathered energy is finally released: a sequence of several dramatic ‘iya’ kakegoe from the ōtsuzumi, and a syncopated pattern, set Sanbasō into motion. He rises to one knee and, over the still-driving percussion, performs a sequence of very rapid kata while chanting loudly and with great energy — his words a joyous, unguarded exclamation: “Oh! What joy! How joyous! Here we have delight! Here happiness for all!”

Then, right before the shōdan's final two words, the driving pattern cuts to sudden silence, and Sanbasō freezes into a static pose — a brief, tense moment created by the sheer disappearance of the percussion that had filled the section until now. He chants those last two words entirely unaccompanied and with minimal movement, punctuated only with a single energetic swirl of both sleeves that leaves him holding his arms in front of him and with his voice rising dramatically on the final syllable.

As he holds this powerful position in silence, four sharp, sparse strokes of the ōtsuzumi resound alone — one last moment of anticipation before the major instrumental dance shōdan, Mominodan. This balance between the shōdan's overwhelming, near-constant density and its few moments of near-total emptiness is what gives Sanbasō's closing words their weight: “wa omō” — “I do believe” — proclaimed with a conviction as strong as the contrast that frames it.
