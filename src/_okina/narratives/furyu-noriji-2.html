Synopsis
As the jiutai of kyōgen actors sitting on the bridge sing the einori chant, Pine and Bamboo dance.
Text
Irregular rhythm. The text, set in einori is sung in tsuyogin style by the jiutai.

Index of Intermedia

The Index of Intermedia (IoI) of this fourth and last dan of the Furyū consists of a single shōdan, which substantially raises the IoI.

This shōdan features an ōnori chant performed by Pine and the jiutai, accompanied by the taiko, ōtsuzumi, and lead kotsuzumi in strict rhythmic setting. As the play’s fifth dance-to-text section, it also incorporates stage movement and serves as the highlight of the Furyū, bringing the IoI to the dan’s highest level and the play’s third highest overall.


As the play's fifth dance-to-text section and the climax of the Furyū, this shōdan reaches the dan's highest Index of Intermedia — and the play's third highest overall — as chant, percussion, and mirrored kata act together for the entire length of the section. The kotsuzumi and ōtsuzumi maintain the same static one-honji pattern employed in the entrance music and the Issei. Here, however, they are joined by the taiko, which embellishes their repetitive pattern with patterns of its own, primarily focused on the honji's initial four beats.

Against this steady percussive backdrop, Pine and Bamboo — positioned on Squares 2 and 6 respectively, while Senzaifuryū remains at Square 8 — execute mirrored kata throughout the chant.

The chant itself carries the section's meaning: Pine and Bamboo affirm their bond in the symbolism of ten thousand years of prosperity, and celebrate a reign at peace with joyous days ahead. Though the text is mostly set to the onori pattern at one syllable per beat, some of its most meaningful words break that regularity, stretched over two or three beats to draw attention to their message.

The word ‘en’, from the sentence “Chitose no matsu no en ni hikarete,” is extended over two beats to emphasize the special and mysterious connection of the visitors with the thousand-year Pine. Likewise, the word ‘miyo’, from “nao masariyuku miyo nakeba,” is extended for three beats to underline the reference to the reign, or time, of the current emperor, said to be joyous and at peace for thousands of years.

In two further instances, this same technique of textual emphasis is augmented by matching kata, deepening the intermedia effect. Upon hearing the jiutai commenting on the bond between Bamboo and Pine with the words “Drawn by a connection to the thousand year,” Pine and Bamboo perform in sync, as if to illustrate their bond, a sequence of six kata: Raise arms, Open fan, Rotate to Face front, Circle point, Forward point, and Open-retreat. Then, with the text “raise their sleeves aloft, hold them high,” they swirl their left sleeves and position them overhead.

The idiom "kazashi kazashite" — extended on its first vowel ‘ka’ — alludes, within the ritual context of Okina, to emerging auspicious signs. At that same moment, Pine and Bamboo, holding their swirled left sleeves above their heads, raise their fans and bring them to their chests. Word and gesture, timed together, reinforce each other, conveying a shared impression of profound appreciation and faith — the fullest expression of chant, percussion, and movement acting as one, forming Furyū's intermedia peak.
