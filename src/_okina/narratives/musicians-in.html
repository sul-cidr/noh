Form
Okina – Procession:
Watari → Zatsuki-bue
Synopsis
Following the oshirabe, the performance opens in near-silence with a slow, ritualistic procession led by Senzai, a young boy carrying the sacred mask box, followed by the Okina-to-be, an elderly man. At center stage, the Okina-to-be performs a solo ritual of deliberate movements and a profound bow to sanctify the space. Senzai then presents the mask box to him, after which the arrival of the Sanbasō actor and the musicians completes the introductory scene.

Visuals
The performance opens with a solemn procession across the hashigakari bridge. Senzai leads, carrying the Okina mask in a casket, followed by the elderly Okina-to-be. Taking center stage, the Okina-to-be performs a formal solo ritual of precise movements and deep bows. After Senzai presents the casket to him, the actor who will portray Sanbasō arrives, with the musicians following to complete the opening scene.

This opening sequence has the lowest Index of Intermedia (IoI) as there is no music or text, the action is almost purely visual and unfolds in near-complete silence. Despite this, the scene is crucial for establishing the solemn, ritualistic context of the play. The slow, deliberate movements create a powerful sense of gravity and focus, setting the stage for the sacred events to follow.

Senzai, carrying Okina’s mask in a casket, opens the performance by entering the stage. He takes his position at Square 3, a location signifying the great value and importance of the casket he carries 3’28”.

Following Senzai is an elderly man, who will subsequently put on the mask and transform into the deity Okina. He pauses at the center of the stage, Square 9, underscoring his elevated rank 4’24”. As he prepares to execute a ritual in deference to the audience and patrons, the remaining actors and musicians remain seated on the hashigakari bridge, refraining from entering the main stage 5’24”. This spatial separation heightens the focus on the lone actor, reinforcing the scene's minimalist, visual-centric nature.

Thereafter, the elderly man deliberately performs the sa-yū kata, a sequence of left-and-right movements culminating in a bow. He executes this kata by synchronizing the movements of the left arm and foot when turning to the left 4’41”, and mirroring this action on the right side 4’52”. The initial turn to the left is an acknowledgement of the higher-ranking side of the stage. This custom is attributed to the imperial system of ancient China, wherein the Emperor, facing south, deemed the east (his left) the more auspicious direction.

This kata sequence is repeated twice before he bows to the audience. The bow is a fundamental aspect of Japanese etiquette, embodying profound respect 5’31”. It is through this gesture that the audience and patrons are recognized. Furthermore, the Noh stage itself is regarded as a unique, and at times sacred, location. Performers commonly enter the stage with a sense of gravitas, and the bow serves to delineate the boundary between the temporal world and the dramatic realm of the play. Upon completion of this ritual, the elderly man leaves his position at center stage and kneels at Square 7 5’24”. This action produces the only sound in the scene: a brief, percussive noise as his knees strike the stage. In the stark silence, this singular sound punctuates the ritual, lending an undeniable power and physical presence to his character.

Subsequently, Senzai brings the casket to the elderly man. After bowing to the elderly man 9’57”, Senzai executes a sa-yū kata 10’02” prior to moving to Square 3. At this point, the individual who will embody the Sanbasō character proceeds to Square 1, followed by the musicians who then take their places on the stage. Their entry signals the end of this silent, preparatory phase and the imminent introduction of music and chanting, which will progressively raise the Index of Intermedia.
