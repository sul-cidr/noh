The choreography of this instrumental dance is structured around the ringing of the suzu bell.

The Suzu (bell) dance is not just a performance, but a ritualistic offering and a powerful expression of blessing and purification. The ringing of the bells is believed to cleanse the space and create a sacred atmosphere. By purifying the space, the dance opens the way for good fortune, prosperity, and longevity. The sound of the bells invites benevolent spirits and deities to bestow blessings upon the world. The Suzu dance connects the mortal realm with the divine, where the performer, often seen as a conduit between worlds, offers the dance as a prayer and a supplication to the gods. It’s a captivating blend of movement, sound, and spiritual intention.

The dance is composed of five distinct parts, articulated by the nohkan's patterns. The length of each part and subsection, measured in honji, is indicated in parentheses below. Each section is accessible by clicking on its respective name:

  • SECTION II: JI-E(5) – JI-F(5) – JI-G(5) – JI-H(5) – TE-B(13)
  • KUDAKI (4)
  • TOME (11)

Compared to the momi no dan, the nohkan's pattern in the suzu no dan is more static. Following the five-honji Donato opening, the nohkan plays a sequence twice, comprised of four five-honji ji-patterns, followed by a thirteen-honji te-pattern. After a four-honji Kudaki-pattern, the nohkan's solo concludes with an eleven-honji Tome-pattern.

The nohkan's solo might be perceived as static because elements of the five-honji Ji-patterns reappear in other sections. The Ji itself has a structure of aabcd, where the second honji is a repetition of the first. Notably, the first three honji of the Ji are also used as the first three of the Te, the first two as the Kudaki's first two, and the very first honji appears as the first five honji of the Tome. This reuse of musical phrases creates a sense of unity and cyclical movement within the dance.

Donato

During the Donato, Sanbasō moves from upstage to Square 4 before arriving at center stage, Square 9. As in the Momi no dan, the three kotsuzumi players introduce a one-honji pattern that is repeated throughout the dance. This pattern features a stroke on every other beat: 1, 3, 5, and 7. The ōtsuzumi, for its part, also has a repetitive pattern, primarily emphasizing even beats: 1, 2, 4, and 6.

This section acts as a pickup to the dance proper, where the tempo increases from ♩= 20 to ♩ = 215. The Donato's tempo steadily accelerates during its first four honji, moving from ♩ = 80 to ♩ = 96, building momentum. This culminates in a substantial decelerando for the fifth and final honji, with ♩ = 48, leading into Section I's opening tempo of ♩ = 20.

Section I

From the beginning of Section I's first Ji until near the piece's end, Sanbasō strikes the suzu bell on every other beat (1, 3, 5, and 7), synchronized with the kotsuzumi's po strokes.

Figure 21

While stamping and sleeve swirling characterized the Momi no dan, the Suzu no dan dance is choreographed around the ringing of the suzu bell. The Sanbasō rings the bell while holding it at four different positions: below knee level, at hip level, at arm's length, and at a raised, extended arm's length. This creates a dynamic transformation of mood, from subdued for the first position to dynamically intense for the last. Furthermore, the ringing of the bell is sometimes intensified by accompanying it with stamping. Expression is also modulated by the Sanbasō's position on the stage. As expected, stamping with extended arms at the front of the stage is more intense than ringing the bell held below knee level upstage.

Starting at Ji-a1, Sanbasō, standing on Square 9, rings the bell, holding it at arm's length, then decreases tension at Ji-a3, holding it below knee level. At Ji-b1, stamping is added to the choreography, a move supported by a change in the ōtsuzumi's pattern, which introduces a three-honji pattern that is clearly perceptible due to its fewer strokes and quasi-rhythmic synchronicity with the kotsuzumi:

Figure 22

At Ji-c5, standing at Square 3 and holding the bell at a raised, extended arm's length, Sanbasō performs a series of stamps while the ōtsuzumi returns with its three-honji pattern. This combined sequence of kata increases the dance's momentum, which is repeated at the beginning of Te-a1, as the Sanbasō stands front center stage, on Square 4. Over Section I, the ♩ modestly increases from ♩ = 20 to ♩ = 50.

Section II

The same three-honji sequence is used at the beginning of Section II, but the effect is more subdued because Sanbasō is standing upstage at Square 1, holding the bell below knee level.

However, this respite is temporary. Momentum is once again built up when the same sequence is repeated at Ji-h4, as Sanbasō is center stage at Square 9. Moreover, an increase in the number of stamps leads to a hip-level hold at Te-b1 and a raised, extended arm at Te-b3.

At Te-b5 and Te-b9, the ōtsuzumi brings back the last two honji of its special pattern, also paired with stamping. Section II's overall increase in intensity is further accentuated by an acceleration of its tempo from ♩ = 72 at the beginning of Section II to ♩ = 134 at the beginning of the Te section.

Kudaki and Tome

Stamping is introduced for the last time during the final two Kudaki's honji and the Tome's first two, with Sanbasō standing center stage. Overall, the number of kata diminishes as the tempo continues to accelerate from Kudaki-1 (♩ = 190) to Tome-9 (♩ = 215). This acceleration is further enhanced by the nohkan's accelerando of its Ho-hi pattern, spread over the entire Tome section. The dance’s is then rapidly brought to a close during the Tome’s last two honji, with the tempo rapidly decreasing from Tome–10 (♩ = 140) and Tome–11 (♩ = 54). Finally, the nohkan dramatically brings the dance to a conclusion with two Hishigi tones.
