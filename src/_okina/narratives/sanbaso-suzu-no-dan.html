Index of Intermedia

The overall Index of Intermedia (IoI) of the third and last dan of the second act consists of two shōdan, maintaining the IoI at its highest level for most of the section.

The first shōdan is an instrumental dance, accompanied by the ōtsuzumi and three kotsuzumi drums in a strict rhythmic setting, and a congruent nohkan part. As the last of five instrumental dances in Okina, it temporarily forgoes verbal expression in favor of choreographic refinement, thereby raising the IoI to its peak.


Following the unaccompanied dialogue of the Mondō—outwardly quiet but charged with the tension of a ritual quarrel—the final shōdan of the act, the Suzunodan, erupts. As a purely instrumental dance, it raises the Index of Intermedia to its absolute peak. This is not just a performance but a climactic ritual offering. The suzu (bell tree), an indispensable shamanistic tool, is the central object; its ringing is widely interpreted as a sonic prayer for a bountiful harvest, purifying the space and inviting blessings from the divine.

The shōdan opens with a transitional musical section, its Nohkan phrases directly imitating the introductory section of Kagura—a Noh piece representing shamanic dance—which immediately frames the performance as a sacred rite. As Senzai exits, Sanbasō prepares. He moves to the front and then back to the center, where he slows and performs a tappai—a deep bow where he leans forward, covering his masked face with a fully open fan. This gesture, which he repeats at the end of the dance, formally echoes the reverent bows of Okina (White Mask), creating a structural link between the two otherwise contrasting deities. The dance proper begins with the piercing hishigi pattern of the nohkan. Initially, the tempo is slow and deliberate. The nohkan plays evenly spaced notes against which Sanbasō shakes the suzu on the offbeats, in perfect sync with the kotsuzumi drums. His movements are meticulous; he leans low, shaking the bell close to the stage as if sowing seeds directly into the earth.

As the music and dance begin a long, steady acceleration, the intermedia expression intensifies. Sanbasō rises, lifting the fan and suzu high above his head while introducing loud, resonant stamps. This marks the start of a new dynamic phase where he systematically purifies the four cardinal directions, performing his core purifying gestures at Squares 9, 5, 3, and 1. Throughout this sequence, the dance is built on a cycle of gathering and releasing power: after a period of high energy, Sanbasō returns to the low, forward-leaning posture, briefly containing the energy before rising again to release it with even greater force as the tempo continues to build.

The final section brings the ritual to its climax. As the tempo accelerates to an extreme, Sanbasō appears possessed. Occupying the center line of the stage, the density of the performance grows to its peak; the stamping can occur on nearly every beat, creating an almost overwhelming feeling of kinetic power and sound. Sanbasō performs a series of powerful contrasts: he stamps vigorously while ringing the bell high and holding the fan low, then reverses the gesture, raising the fan and lowering the bell while ceasing the stamps. After a final circle with arms outstretched, he returns to the center for one last powerful sequence of stomps. The ritual then concludes. Following a final circle, two nohkan hishigi cut through the texture, initiating a dramatic slowdown. This final musical passage is aptly named Suzu-kudaki ("Shattering of the Bells"), evoking both the memory of the furious shaking that just occurred and the symbolic scattering of sacred seeds for a future harvest. The ōtsuzumi falls silent, and to this sparse accompaniment, Sanbasō drops to his knees, stretches his arms out one last time, and brings the fan and bell together in a final tappai, reverent bow, sealing the blessing.
