Index of Intermedia

The overall Index of Intermedia (IoI) of the first dan of the second act consists of three shōdan, maintaining the IoI at its highest level for most of the section.

The dan’s second shōdan is an instrumental dance, accompanied by the ōtsuzumi and three kotsuzumi drums in a strict rhythmic setting, and a congruent nohkan part. As the fourth of five instrumental dances in Okina, it temporarily forgoes verbal expression in favor of choreographic refinement, thereby raising the IoI to its peak.


Following the tense stillness at the end of Momidashi, the Mominodan erupts as the first of Sanbasō’s two instrumental dances. As a purely instrumental dance, its Index of Intermedia is at its maximum. Its primary function is seen as the ritual preparation and pacification of the earth. This is a kinetic prayer enacted primarily through two fundamental actions: powerful, repetitive stamping (ashi-byōshi) presumably to firm the ground for planting, and wide circling ostensibly to spread the divine blessing, with each action driven and amplified by the music’s matching energy. At nearly eight minutes, it is an extended showcase of divine power channeled through the uninhibited physicality of a Kyōgen actor. The result is a performance of such extravagant vigor—with its relentless stamping, jumps, speed, and shouted calls—that, when contrasted with the sublime control of the Noh-performed Okina, it borders on sacred parody.

The shōdan begins slowly, with the music gradually accelerating to establish a powerful, driving pulse. In the opening rounds, Sanbasō remains mostly in place, his movements sparse but powerful. While his arms remain outstretched—a posture held over from the end of Momidashi that holds the building energy in a state of powerful stasis—his legs begin the ritual. He marks the music at first with single, resonant stamps, then steadily increases their frequency, building a kinetic energy that is visibly contained by his upper body. The dance then unfolds in six rounds, each built on a pattern of stamping, circling, and arm gestures. The first two rounds establish a leftward, purifying orientation, followed by an alternating sequence of right and left circles. This structure creates a dynamic tension: the vigorous, downward-driving energy of the stamps is repeatedly released into the expansive, horizontal energy of the walked circles.

This energetic breathing is mirrored perfectly in the music. The percussion is grounded in the kotsuzumi’s steady “one-honji” pattern, providing a consistent pulse. In contrast to its complex role in Momidashi, the ōtsuzumi’s function here is to directly support the dance, with its “chon” strokes on even beats complementing the kotsuzumi’s odd beats. Crucially, the dramatic ‘iya’ kakegoe from the ōtsuzumi are present only during the stamping sequences, intensifying the moments of action and falling away during the quieter circling. This interplay is further amplified by Sanbasō's vocalizations. In a practice highly unusual for a Noh performer, the dancer shouts his own powerful kakegoe (‘iya,’ ‘ha’), weaving his own voice directly into the rhythmic fabric and heightening the dance's raw, invigorating atmosphere.

The dance builds to a powerful climax. The fifth round features a moment of intense stamping directly in front of the audience at Square 4, and the final round unleashes the dance's full power. Sanbasō executes a series of , stamping forcefully toward the audience as he moves toward Square 3 in the most impressive display of vigor yet. The ending, however, brings a sudden shift. The propulsive energy lowers as Sanbasō walks slowly backward to Square 8. In a unique moment of unity, he shouts a final kakegoe 'iya' in unison with all the musicians before dropping with a loud stomp onto his right knee. This final, decisive action brings the explosive energy to a controlled and powerful conclusion, leaving the stage ritually prepared and resonating with divine force.
