Form
Okina-Exit:
Okina-Gaeri
Synopsis
The final dan of the Okina act portrays the deity's departure and his transformation back into an old man. After ceremoniously removing the mask, the performer offers a final bow to the audience and gods. His exit from the stage is a deliberate, ritualized reversal of his entrance. The accompanying music builds from near-silence to an unusual final acceleration, signaling that while Okina's part is over, the larger ceremony of the play is not yet complete.

Visuals
The performer, soon to turn back to be an old man, walks to Square 7 and removes Okina's mask. He then moves slowly to Square 8, performs a very slow variation of a closure scoop kata 37’07”, and offers a final bow 37’44”. His entire exit from the stage is the Okina-gaeri, a reversal of his entrance kata, featuring right-left-right turns and kneeling on the right knee. This slow, dignified walk continues across the bridge until he is fully offstage and the curtain closes behind him, concluding the first act.
Index of Intermedia

The Index of Intermedia (IoI) of this sixth and final dan of the first act consists of a single shōdan, which lowers the IoI to a low level.

This shōdan features three kotsuzumi drums in strict rhythmic setting, supporting Okina as he exits the stage. The lower IoI reflects the shōdan’s cadential character.


This exit dan has a relatively low Index of Intermedia, focusing on a highly ceremonial action supported by minimal music. The momentum, however, builds in a long, continuous acceleration from near-silence to a climactic rush. The dan begins with extremely sparse percussion—single beats every eight seconds or so—creating a sober atmosphere that focuses all attention on Okina as he walks to the mask box and removes the mask. As the performer, no longer a diety, begins his exit, the almost imperceptible acceleration of the kotsuzumi strikes coalesces into a more defined metric pattern. The energy increases markedly when he reaches the bridge, as the nohkan enters with a simple Hi-yo-ra melody named iwai-no-te (“melody of celebration”) at 39’08” to underline the departure. At the bridge’s midpoint, the percussion pattern intensifies further, adding energetic pick-up strikes before each main beat. The acceleration continues to build until it is broken abruptly only after he is offstage and the curtain has closed. This unexpected surge of energy is exceptional for a Noh ending and suggests that the ritual is not yet over, creating powerful anticipation for what is to follow.
