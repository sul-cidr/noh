Form
Okina-Okina Performs:
Dance-1 → Dance-2 → Dance-3
Synopsis
Okina and the jiutai engage in a repetitive chant, calling out "Manzairaku"—a wish for ten thousand years of happiness. The dance itself is minimal and introspective; rather than performing the celebratory gagaku dance of the same name, the regular dance-like percussion and Okina’s dignified presence evokes its spirit. The entire three-part dance dan culminates with the performance of the third and final symbolic gesture, the jin-no-tappai, or “paying respect to Man.”
Text
Arhythmic. The text, set in sashinori is sung in tsuyogin style by Okina and the jiutai.
Music
The three kotsuzumi are in strict rhythmic settings.
Visuals
The kata are limited and spatially contained, unfolding primarily upstage at Square 8. The dance is strikingly static; Okina performs simple turning movements, at times watching the musicians, as if inviting the audience to imagine the grand "Manzairaku" dance. The shōdan, and the entire dance, concludes with Okina performing the final symbolic kata: he raises his closed arms and veils his face with his fan, holding a still, reflective pose.

The Index of Intermedia recedes to a slightly lower (but still high) level in this final dance-to-text shōdan, and as the nohkan is now silent. The shōdan functions as an extended cadential section for the entire three-part dance. The hayashi ensemble is limited to the three kotsuzumi, which perform a simple, repetitive one-honji pattern that emphasizes beats 1, 3, 5, and 7—a rhythm first heard in Okina’s Song. Although the tempo is MM = 90, the emphasis on every other beat creates a slower perceived tempo of MM = 45, with a half-beat pickup providing subtle momentum. A striking contrast emerges between the active, dance-like percussion and Okina's stately, minimal movements. The text itself is a repetitive call-and-response between Okina and the jiutai chant of "Manzairaku," a term signifying 'Ten Thousand Years of Happiness' and associated with a celebratory gagaku dance. This contrast implies that the dance is not being performed literally; instead, its spirit is evoked in the imagination, embodied by Okina’s dignified presence. As the music slows to a close, Okina performs the final symbolic gesture of the dan: the jin-no-tappai or "Man" kata consisting of bringing his arms together to cover the face with an open fan. He simultaneously shifts to the melodious yowagin vocal style, bringing the performance to a solemn conclusion.
