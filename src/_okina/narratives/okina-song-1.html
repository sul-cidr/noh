Synopsis
Okina’s song-1 is the first work in the Section’s second subsection. The first line of Okina-to-be and the jiutai are set in a lost language, that contributes to give this song a spiritual tone. The rest of the text is a blessing, invoking the tortoise and crane since these animals are emblems of auspicious, long-lasting blessings.
Text
Arhythmic. The text, set in sashinori is sung in tsuyogin style by Okina-to-be and the jiutai.
Music
The three kotsuzumi are in strict, and the nohkan in non-congruent rhythmic settings.
Visuals
The song is delivered over a standstill stage, since Okina-to-be remains seated at Square 7 as he delivers his line.

Okina’s initial song exhibits a ritualistic character, stemming from the fact that the semantic content of its text has been lost to time. It is hypothesized that the text may represent an ancient song, archaic shōga, or even Sanskrit. Due to the absence of discernible meaning, the text is perceived primarily as a sequence of onomatopoeic sounds. It is rendered by Okina in alternation with the jiutai. The song’s ritualistic character is further emphasized by the absence of any physical movement, as Okina remains seated throughout the performance.

The song commences at measure 10, at which point the nohkan joins with its Hachō no yuri pattern, a composition of six continuous phrases. The combined effect of the song and the nohkan part creates a seamless continuum, a distinct layer juxtaposed against the pulsating patterns of the three kotsuzumi.

The percussion instruments introduce three distinct eight-beat patterns. The first pattern is predicated upon two distinct sounds: the ‘ta’ and ‘po’ strokes, with the former employed in the first half of the pattern and the latter in the second half. It comprises a sequence of two-beat strokes articulated on beats 1, 3, 5, and 7, its regularity broken only by a stroke on beat 6.5 that serves as an anacrusis, or pick-up, to beat 7, thereby accentuating it. Thus, the first half contains two strokes, while the second half contains three.

The second pattern is initially introduced in measures 6 and 7, anticipating the entrance of the nohkan and Okina’s melody by four honji. It is readily discernible due to the lead kotsuzumi player performing a pattern slightly divergent from those executed by the other two players.

Furthermore, this pattern is more dynamically complex than the first, featuring strokes on beats 1, 3, and 4 in the first half, and 5, 6, 6.5, and 7 in the second half, for a total of seven strokes compared to the first pattern's five.

This pattern is reiterated at measure 20, four measures prior to the section's conclusion, which is itself identified with this pattern.
