Form
Okina-Encounter:
Encounter
Synopsis
This is the first work of the section’s fourth subsection. The focus returns to Okina, who meets Sanbasō-to-be, but the jiutai speaks for him since he is not yet a deity.
Text
Arhythmic. The text, set in sashinori is sung in tsuyogin style by Okina and the jiutai.
Music
The three kotsuzumi are in strict, and the nohkan in non-congruent rhythmic settings.
Visuals
For the most part, Okina and Sanbasō-to-be facing each other, remain seated at their respective distant Square 7 and 1.

In this section, the tempo slows to ♩ = 54, providing a respite following the dynamic preceding songs and dances performed by Senzai. This slower tempo also lends a ceremonial quality to the scene as Okina encounters his counterpart, Sanbasō. Although they establish eye contact, they do not directly exchange any words; rather, the text is sung by Okina in alternation with the jiutai, who speaks on behalf of Sanbasō. The text is relatively mundane, with the two characters acknowledging each other's presence.

Despite the limited physical motion in this section, three significant observations can be made:

  1. Upon entering the stage to sing and dance, Senzai did not establish contact with either Okina or Sanbasō. With Okina and Sanbasō facing each other, this marks the first interaction between two characters, even if the jiutai vocalizes for the latter.
  2. Okina delivered his preceding two songs while seated, thereby freeing the stage for Senzai’s dance. In contrast, as the jiutai sings “I will stand now and go to you, tondoya!” 20’41”, Okina and Sanbasō-to-be rise to a standing position. After moving two steps toward each other, Sanbasō-to-be proceeds to the upstage, while Okina moves to Square 8. The play’s focus has now demonstrably shifted to Okina.
  3. For his final motion, Okina extends his arms and brings his hands together, consequently covering his face with his opened fan, establishing a reflective mood that is well-suited for the subsequent prayer.

This section incorporates both nohkan and percussion parts. The nohkan enters on the second half of the second measure’s fourth beat 19’25” with the Notare no ryō pattern. Its part is comprised of five short motifs, each averaging three shōga: Ho ro, Tsu ro i ya, Ro ru ra, Ho hi ya, Chi yo u. None of these are repeated, thus creating a continuum that contrasts with the steady percussion pattern.

The section’s initial two measures serve as a percussion introduction that leads to a one-honji pattern. Specifically, from measures 3 – 11, the three kotsuzumi introduce the one-honji rhythmic pattern, with strokes on all beats. The parts between the leader and the two other performers are distinct, identified as A and B in the figure below. Consequently, they alternate parts every measure as depicted, thereby creating a combined two-honji pattern that is quite rich in timbre.
