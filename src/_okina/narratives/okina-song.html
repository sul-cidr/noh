Form
Okina-Introductory Song:
Shiku-no-kamiuta
Synopsis
The performance transitions into the second dan, sung by the Okina actor and the jiutai. The opening line is an incantation without specific meaning that establishes a mysterious and ritualistic tone. The song then continues with a blessing that invokes the tortoise and crane—traditional emblems of longevity. In a call-and-response, the Okina-to-be and chorus repeat the incantation, but this time set it against a dramatically different accompaniment. A simple, driving drum ostinato accelerates to an extreme tempo, generating powerful energy on an otherwise motionless stage and marking the climax of the shōdan.
Text
Regular text set in sashinori, is sung in tsuyogin style by Okina and the jiutai.
Music
The three kotsuzumi are in strict, and the nohkan in non-congruent rhythmic settings.
Visuals
The stage is motionless, as the performer who will become Okina sings his part while seated at Square 7.
Index of Intermedia

The Index of Intermedia (IoI) of this second dan—which consists of a single shōdan—rises to a mid-level.

This non-congruent chant, performed by Okina and the jiutai and accompanied by three kotsuzumi drums in a strict rhythmic setting and a non-congruent nohkan part, marks a significant increase in the IoI, serving as a bridge from the lowest level of the first dan to the third dan, which will feature some of the highest levels found among the first act’s shōdan.


In Shiku-no-kamiuta, the performance shifts from the solo nohkan to a dance-to-text with a full ensemble, significantly raising the Index of Intermedia. Its ritualistic character is established by the opening lines. Their sounds, said to derive from a form of solfege, or onomatopoeia that imitates the sound of percussion or wind instruments, are sung like a spell, without forming words. Interestingly, the same sound "to-u or tō" is referenced in the immediately following Senzai Song 1 as an onomatopoeia for a thundering waterfall 12’58”. The music builds in layers, beginning with the percussive power of the three kotsuzumi drums playing in rhythmic unison 11’56”. After four repetitions at a fast tempo, they deliberately slow down 12’40”, creating an opening for the vocals and the nohkan flute, leading to a call-and-response between the Okina-to-be and the chorus 12’48”.

The ritual text is then repeated, but its intensity is amplified by the musical accompaniment—a heightened expression that may suggest the shamanistic possession of the character. The three kotsuzumi establish a new ostinato pattern that undergoes an extreme acceleration 14’28”. A doubling of tempo generates immense energy, creating the most dynamic experience of the performance so far. The shōdan is audibly framed by the piercing Hishigi flute pattern at both its opening and conclusion. While the performers remain physically motionless, the stage is activated sonically, by the vocal alternation and the intense drums.
