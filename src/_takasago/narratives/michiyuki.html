Form
First Act - Waki Enters:
Netori Okitsuzumi → Kaikō → Nanori → Shidai chant → Michiyuki → Tsuki-zerifu
Synopsis
The priest and his two attendants make their way to the capital and soon arrive at their destination.
Text
Poetry in regular rhythm.
Music
The chant is sung in tsuyogin style set in hiranori. It is accompanied by the percussion in strict rhythmic setting and a non-congruent nohkan part
Visuals
The waki and the two wakizure face each other are still standing at Square 5 and Squares 2 and 3, respectively. The waki turns and faces front to announce that they have now arrived at Takasago Bay in Banshū.
Index of Intermedia

The overall Index of Intermedia (IoI) of this first dan, which comprises six shōdan, forms an inverted parabolic pattern: the fourth and fifth shōdan exhibit the highest indexes, whereas the surrounding ones display the lowest.

The fifth shōdan also features a hiranori chant sung by the waki and wakizure, and jiutai accompanied by two drums in strict rhythmic setting, and a non-congruent nohkan part. As a result, the IoI remains at the same level—the third highest in the first act.


This shōdan is essentially an ageuta, but with an unusually energetic character suited to a traveling song. It follows the traditional ageuta design by beginning with a line that is then repeated, separated by a percussion uchikiri; here it is sung in unison by the waki and wakizure, while the nohkan enhances the first uchikiri, a two-measure one, with a special nohkan hishigi takane pattern that includes a high hishigi note, evoking a peaceful voyage across the sea. The opening tori, with its truncated first line, further propels the sense of motion.

In the second half, unlike most ageuta, the mitsuji pattern appears only once and gives way to a continuous tsuzuke; instead of a contrasting uchikiri to reset the tempo, the second uchikiri uses a special pattern that articulates almost every beat and adds energetic kakegoe (vocal calls), leading to the subsequent vocal lines with even greater momentum. This heightened musical energy aligns with the waki’s stage movements: a sequence of kata culminating in the demonstrative gesture “Catch the Front,” followed by an unusual foot pattern known as tsukiashi, which signals arrival at the shore of Takasago. The customary repetition of the final line reinforces the sense of arrival poetically and musically.
